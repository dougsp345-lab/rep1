# Learning design

## Purpose

Phonics Path gives learners a clear route from beginning sound awareness to increasingly flexible word decoding. Common patterns come first so that useful reading starts early. The advanced sequence extends coverage to uncommon correspondences and teaches how to resolve ambiguity.

“Decode anything” is an educational aspiration, not a literal rule guarantee. English has no closed, exception-free list that uniquely supplies the pronunciation of every word. Context distinguishes heteronyms such as *read* and *wind*; unfamiliar names and borrowings can require a reference. A capable reader can make a reasoned first attempt, notice uncertainty, and verify it.

## Course organization

The lesson data is the authoritative inventory of 120 lessons. The instructional order follows these broad bands; individual lessons keep each new target small.

| Band | Main work | Why it belongs here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readiness | Hear and blend spoken sounds; connect print with speech | Establish what a sound is and what reading asks the learner to do |
| First words | A small useful set of consonants, short vowels, and simple CVC words | Begin blending before waiting to learn every letter |
| Common consonants | Remaining common consonants, digraphs, clusters, and basic endings | Expand the range of readily decodable words |
| Long vowels and common teams | Split digraphs, common vowel teams, and frequent alternatives | Cover a substantial portion of everyday reading patterns |
| Other common vowel patterns | R-influenced vowels, diphthongs, and common variant spellings | Build flexible sound–spelling knowledge |
| Longer words | Syllable structures, compounds, affixes, and inflections | Apply known code to words beyond one syllable |
| Advanced code | Additional vowel and consonant correspondences, silent letters, and less common patterns | Extend the learner's repertoire without overloading the early course |
| Flexible decoding | Schwa, stress, morphology, borrowed spellings, heteronyms, and verification | Recognize where pronunciation needs more than a first phonics attempt |

High-frequency exception words belong where they enable meaningful reading; they need not wait until the advanced course. Teach the regular parts and identify the unexpected part. Do not describe all letters in an exception word as arbitrary.

The exact order is an authored curriculum choice. Neither a particular letter order nor the number of lessons is evidence that one program is uniquely optimal. Progress through small prerequisites and the quality of examples matter more than the appearance of a long syllabus.

## A lesson's teaching cycle

1. **Explain and model.** State the pattern plainly, show an example, and identify the relevant letters. A capable reader supplies pure letter sounds when needed.
2. **Practise with support.** Work through examples with visible help. Assistance is useful instruction, but not independent mastery evidence.
3. **Retrieve independently.** Read a word aloud before revealing its pronunciation, or spell a heard word without seeing its answer. Choices may provide additional practice, but are not sufficient for mastery.
4. **Correct specifically.** Show the missed pattern or word, model the correct response, and let the learner try again. A corrected answer is not retroactively a correct first attempt.
5. **Revisit.** Mix previously taught material into later practice and return after a delay.

Connected text helps the learner use decoding in meaningful reading. Choose sentences aligned with taught patterns and explain new vocabulary. An illustration can enrich a story after reading; it should not give away a word that the child is meant to decode. Reading aloud and discussing books with an adult remains useful alongside the application.

## Mastery and review

The engine separates recognition practice from independent spelling and adult-checked reading. Its initial mastery heuristic normally requires at least eight independent correct responses, with every word in the lesson's bank answered independently at least once through spelling or checked reading. It also requires at least two distinct words read with an adult check, at least two distinct words spelled, and at least 80% correct across the recent eligible responses. A repeated success on one easy word cannot replace coverage of the other words in that lesson.

Where a lesson cannot supply at least two unambiguous audio-spelling candidates, the reading-only fallback requires every bank word to be read correctly with an adult check, at least eight checked successes overall, and at least six distinct words (or the entire bank if it contains fewer than six). It retains the recent-accuracy requirement. A later successful checked reading attempt at least 24 hours after initial qualification is needed for the stronger “secure” status. Oral-readiness activities have their own adult-checked target rather than a spelling requirement.

Review intervals are 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 days. These intervals and the numerical mastery thresholds are transparent software heuristics. They are not norms, validated assessment cutoffs, grade equivalents, or research-established optimal intervals for every learner.

The app records a capable reader's judgment of oral reading; it does not verify that judgment through a microphone. A learner can mistakenly or deliberately overstate performance. An adult should check whether the response was independent, accurate, and applicable to more than the practised example.

Item variety matters. Several successes on one remembered word should not substitute for transfer across a pattern. An unfamiliar real word can test transfer; a carefully selected, clearly labelled pseudoword can additionally reduce reliance on familiar-word memory. Advanced ambiguous spellings require accepted pronunciation alternatives, so not every generated letter string makes a valid assessment item.

## Motivation that serves learning

The central reward is visible growth: a completed short round, more reliable responses, and an expanding record of skills. The learner can continue without a fixed endpoint to practice. Repetition remains purposeful; an endless quiz loop does not imply an infinite bank of unique words.

Learning rounds do not depend on countdown pressure, losing lives, random prizes, social ranking, or punishment for missing a day. Accuracy comes before speed. Brief, successful sessions and a clear stopping point let an adult adjust to fatigue and attention. A learner who repeatedly struggles needs modeling or an easier prerequisite, not more pressure to click through the same task.

This gamification is an informed design choice, not a proven treatment. Engagement, time on task, and points are not interchangeable with reading improvement.

## Audio and dialect

Whole-word browser speech can model an example or provide a post-attempt check. It should not play the answer before an independent reading attempt. Devices differ in available voices and in how they pronounce unusual words and heteronyms. For covered heteronyms, a sentence supplies the intended meaning before reading; a pronunciation guide is revealed for the adult after the attempt. Isolated-word browser audio is disabled for those checks because its pronunciation may not fit the context. Valid dialect and dictionary variants remain acceptable.

Do not feed isolated letters to text-to-speech and call the output a phoneme model: the system may say a letter name or add a vowel. An adult should model continuous sounds cleanly and stop sounds briefly, avoiding an extra “uh.” The application does not use speech recognition to judge young children's pronunciation.

The teaching examples primarily follow American English. Valid dialect differences are not decoding failures. R-influenced vowels, *cot/caught*, *pin/pen*, and *wh/w* may differ. An adult should accept the learner's consistent local pronunciation while checking that the intended word and spelling were identified.

## Coverage and quality checks

Treat coverage as a maintained inventory of correspondence families, not a claim that every dictionary word has been enumerated. Inspect every lesson for its target pattern, prerequisites, explicit explanation, examples, practice items, exceptions, and pronunciation assumptions.

For each addition or revision:

- Check that examples actually demonstrate the stated pattern and do not introduce unexplained prerequisites.
- Check that spelling prompts distinguish homophones or accept legitimate alternatives.
- Check that choices have one defensible answer and do not reveal it through formatting, length, or a picture.
- Check that syllable divisions, affixes, and grapheme highlighting are linguistically defensible.
- Check that a delayed review, a retry, or hearing the answer cannot silently create independent mastery evidence.
- Check profile isolation, saving, backup/import validation, and the scope of reset actions.

Automated checks can verify data shape and progress rules. They cannot establish that every word is pedagogically well chosen, that browser speech sounds correct on every device, or that a child has learned to read. Human content review and observation of learners remain necessary.

## Evidence and provenance

**Systematic, explicit teaching and connected reading.** The [IES/What Works Clearinghouse foundational-reading guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21) rates evidence for sound–letter links and decoding/word analysis as strong, and daily connected-text reading as moderate. Its [implementation guidance](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/midwest/pdf/videos/foundational-skills-guide-508.pdf) emphasizes a planned sequence, modeling, scaffolded practice, feedback, and checks for understanding. These support the teaching cycle, not a guarantee about this app.

**Retrieval and spacing.** [IES guidance on organizing instruction and study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) supports delayed review and retrieval practice. The application's schedule is an implementation of those general principles; the guide does not validate this exact algorithm.

**Multisyllabic words and morphology.** The [IES PRISMS module](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/rel/reading-intervention-grades-6-8/pro-learning-module2) recommends assessing prerequisites, explicitly modeling a word-part routine, embedding spelling, and practising in words and connected text. Its older-student context should not be confused with validation of the entire beginning-reader course.

**Alternatives, exceptions, and broader reading.** The [English national curriculum](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study) includes alternative grapheme sounds, exception words, morphology, and flexible pronunciation attempts. It distinguishes word reading from language comprehension. The [DfE Reading Framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-reading-framework-teaching-the-foundations-of-literacy) supports systematic phonics and accurate sound articulation. British examples require adaptation for American pronunciation.

**Mentava as a product reference.** [Mentava's published curriculum](https://www.mentava.com/curriculum) demonstrates a product approach built around early blending, a structured progression, many active practice activities, and transfer to books. Phonics Path uses general instructional ideas rather than copying proprietary lessons, assets, or branding. Mentava's [trial description](https://www.mentava.com/mentava-trial) includes adult involvement and notes that results and pace vary. Product descriptions and testimonials are not independent efficacy trials.

Phonics Path itself has not been evaluated in a controlled study. It is not a substitute for assessment or individualized teaching when progress is persistently difficult. It also does not attempt to supply the full vocabulary, knowledge, oral language, and comprehension instruction that skilled reading requires.
