# Phonics Path: curriculum scope and coverage

This is an original 120-module curriculum, not a reproduction of Mentava or any other proprietary program. It combines explicit teaching, word-level practice, oral reading, spelling, and delayed review. The application is an instructional tool; it has not itself been tested in a controlled efficacy study.

## Sequence

| Stage | Modules | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| 0 — Hear the sounds | 3 | Adult-led oral blending, segmentation, and sound changes. No print-reading prerequisite barrier. |
| 1 — First words | 12 | Common letter sounds, the whole alphabet, short vowels, CVC blending, and word chains. |
| 2 — Consonant teams | 15 | Digraphs, consonant clusters, common final spellings, and explicitly mapped high-frequency irregular words. |
| 3 — Long vowels | 9 | Open syllables, split vowel patterns, final y, and soft c/g. |
| 4 — The everyday code | 15 | Common vowel teams, diphthongs, and r-controlled spellings. |
| 5 — Longer words | 12 | Inflections, spelling changes, syllable patterns, compounds, and flexible division. |
| 6 — Code alternatives | 21 | Less common vowel values, silent consonants, alternative consonant spellings, and difficult families. |
| 7 — Word structure | 14 | Prefixes, suffixes, Latin and Greek components, stress, schwa, weak syllables, and derived-word changes. |
| 8 — Advanced decoding | 19 | Loanwords, rare spellings, heteronyms, homophones, dialect, names, abbreviations, and reference strategies. |

Stages 1–5 prioritize productive patterns and frequent words. No percentage of English text coverage is asserted: coverage depends on the text, vocabulary, dialect, and how a code is counted. Later stages add less common correspondences and tools for genuine ambiguity. There is no finite, exception-free list that uniquely determines the pronunciation of every English word, name, loanword, abbreviation, or future coinage.

## Code inventory

**Consonants.** All ordinary alphabet consonant values; consonant y; c/k/ck; soft c; hard and soft g; gu/gue; qu as /kw/ and borrowed /k/; x as /ks/, /gz/, or /z/; doubled consonants; sh, ch, th (voiced and unvoiced), ng, n before k; wh; tch/dge; ph/gh as /f/; alternative ch values; ce, sc, ti, ci, si, ssi, sion, tion, ssion, cian, cial, tial, cious, tious; ture/sure and palatalized consonants; s, se, ss and z alternatives; s in sugar/sure; z in azure; silent initial and medial/final consonants; Greek ps/pn/pt/rh; source-language consonant patterns in loans.

**Vowels.** Five short-vowel spellings and regional realizations; open-syllable long vowels; a_e/i_e/o_e/u_e/e_e; both /yoo/ and /oo/ values of long u; ai/ay, ee/ea, oa/ow, igh/ie, ue/ew, oi/oy, ou/ow, aw/au, oo variants; ar/or/ore/er/ir/ur; air/are/ear/eer/ere; ea alternatives; ie/ei alternatives; ei/eigh/ey/aigh for long a; final y/ey; y as short i and long i; i as long e; ou variants and ould; u in put, o in son, oo in blood; a after w/qu; al/all/alk/alm/alt; wor/war; ear/our alternatives; ough/augh families; long vowels in find/child/most/old/roll; ui/eu/ieu/oe/eau/eo/ae; unusual vowel spellings in common historical exceptions; schwa and other weak vowels.

**Beyond single correspondences.** Word endings -s/-es and -ed; -ing; doubling, dropping e, and y-to-i changes; common syllable types; flexible divisions; consonant-le and syllabic consonants; prefixes/suffixes and roots; stress and stress shifts; meaning-family pronunciation changes; contractions; connected-speech reductions; homophones and heteronyms; dialect variation; proper names, initialisms, acronyms, and dictionary use.

This inventory is intentionally broad. The module data, not a marketing claim of “complete rules,” is the auditable statement of what is taught. Some rare forms are taught as individual words because inventing a general rule would mislead.

## Content and use notes

- **Beginning scope.** Stage 1 introduces letters in small sets. Within a lesson, the adult teaches one new letter at a time and uses only already introduced examples until the whole set is known. The first sentence explicitly introduces the common weak word *a*. CVC word banks remain within the taught stage-1 alphabet; ng, nk, sh, ch, and th are introduced before their quiz use. Starting and ending clusters are distinct from digraphs: clusters keep multiple sounds.
- **Examples are spelling chunks.** `parts` provides an instructional segmentation of written strings. It is not an IPA transcription or a guarantee of one segment per phoneme. In particular x and qu commonly represent two sounds; split vowel spellings, meaningful roots, silent letters, and advanced examples need different groupings. The examples explain these distinctions.
- **Oral readiness.** Stage-0 words are for spoken work led by an adult. The child does not need to decode their printed forms. Short oral activities support instruction; they should not indefinitely postpone learning letters and blending.
- **Sentences.** Early sentences mostly reuse taught code and explicitly introduced common words. Adult support remains necessary for meanings and any word whose full pattern has not yet been introduced. Later examples and sentences intentionally include multisyllable vocabulary to illustrate the target pattern; they are not a fully controlled decodable-book corpus. Connected-text reading should accompany this app, with the adult checking that independent text matches the learner's taught code.
- **Word audio.** Browser speech synthesis models whole words and sentences. It does not provide verified isolated phonemes. An adult should model pure consonant sounds without an added schwa, monitor pronunciation, and accept the learner's dialect. Sentence context is essential for heteronyms. A TTS voice can choose the wrong meaning of an isolated word.
- **Spelling tasks.** A heard word can have multiple spellings. Homophones require sentence/meaning context or accepted alternatives. The app should exclude ambiguous isolated dictation items from automatic scoring. Proper names and abbreviations should receive adult-supported treatment. The curriculum's `words` lists are reading banks; not every word is suitable for every quiz mode.
- **Transfer.** Optional pseudowords contain taught simple patterns and an adult pronunciation guide. They must be clearly labeled invented words; do not use TTS as a pronunciation authority. Unseen real words and checked oral reading are also needed to distinguish decoding from memorized answer recognition.
- **Mastery.** Repeated multiple-choice success is insufficient to establish reading skill. Require accurate encoding and checked oral reading across different words, followed by delayed review and transfer. A self-check is practice, not an independently validated assessment.
- **Natural speech.** Do not penalize dialect differences, weak vowels, syllabic consonants, optional consonants, or accepted alternate pronunciations. The code is taught in relation to the learner's spoken language.
- **Scope limit.** Decoding supports reading but is not the whole of reading. Vocabulary, comprehension, oral language, handwriting/encoding, and fluent connected-text reading require ongoing instruction and experience.

## Research alignment and evidence limits

The structure follows broadly supported principles of explicit systematic phonics, phonemic awareness linked to print, decoding/encoding practice, supported connected-text reading, and cumulative retrieval. It does not establish that this exact scope order, mastery threshold, reward system, or app implementation is uniquely best. Parent project documentation supplies primary-source research references. The lesson corpus and wording are newly written and should receive expert review before use as a sole reading curriculum.
